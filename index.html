PMRA — A Falsification Study

A forecasting harness built to test one trading hypothesis, instrumented to measure itself, and used to disprove that hypothesis. This is a research artifact: the null result is the finding.

Falsifiable hypothesis

A web-search LLM can estimate prediction-market outcome probabilities accurately enough to be profitable net of Kalshi's fees and bid-ask spread.

Kill condition: the model must show positive Brier skill (beat a 0.25 coin flip) and positive net-of-fee P&L, in some category, at n ≥ 30. If it can't, the hypothesis is dead and no capital should move.

Result: hypothesis falsified — four independent ways. The market is efficient at retail scale across every tested edge source; the conclusion rests on the statistically-powered 4,610-market behavioral study (the small forecasting sample only raised the question). Total cost to reach a complete, defensible negative: $6.97 in API credits, $0 capital deployed.

The starting anomaly — small sample

On a small sample, the forecasts landed worse than chance — so I built a powered test

The measurement layer I built — not intuition — flagged this. Each point is the model's predicted probability against how often those markets actually resolved YES. This resolved set is small (n≈22), so it is suggestive, not conclusive — which is exactly why the statistically-powered behavioral study further down exists.

Brier 0.305 (n=22)

On this small resolved sample, worse than the 0.25 of a coin flip — suggestive, not settled proof. It raised the question; the powered study answered it.

Why this is the deliverable

Most quant hypotheses fail. The value isn't finding alpha — it's distrusting a small, promising-looking sample, building the powered test to check it, and stopping when the data says stop.

What I built to test it honestly

The measurement harness

A full research loop — but the reusable asset is the scorekeeping, not the forecaster. The recalibrator and the append-only prediction-vs-outcome store were built specifically so the hypothesis could be disproven.

Calibration here is diagnostic infrastructure, not a fix. The temperature-scaling recalibrator never reached its activation threshold (needs 50 resolved pairs; had 22), and temperature scaling corrects confidence miscalibration — it cannot repair a directional skill failure regardless. Its job was to make the failure measurable, and it did.

The part that matters

Two moments of resisting my own motivated reasoning

Both are cases where the easy move was to bank a positive. Neither survived scrutiny, and I said so.

Caught a look-ahead leak

A fake positive, refused

Testing near-dated Politics markets — the one category that looked plausibly inefficient — I realized all 232 resolved candidates closed after the model's training cutoff, so its web search could read the outcome instead of forecasting it. I checked whether it did.

Leak detected in 6 / 6 probed markets · contaminated Brier 0.164 discarded · fell back to the clean free baseline: the market's own price forecasts these at Brier ≈ 0.10 (efficient).
Killed a significant result

t = 2.6, and still dead

Buying deep favorites (0.90–0.99) showed a net positive edge at t = 2.6–2.9 — nominally exploitable. I didn't take it. The entire edge rested on a handful of tail events, so the Gaussian t-statistic is invalid under that payoff skew.

Edge rests on 7 loss events (2 near close) · +0.03 win / −0.95 loss skew invalidates the t-stat · $10 fills at 0.95+ quotes unverifiable. Nominally significant, not real.

Conclusion

The correct action was not to trade

Across forecasting, bias, structure, and behavior, the market is efficient at retail scale, and every apparent exception is either inside the spread or inside the noise. The conclusion rests on the statistically-powered 4,610-market behavioral study — not the small forecasting sample. Recognizing that 13 signals across ~5 correlated events was far too little to conclude anything is exactly why that powered study exists: a deliberate self-correction, not an afterthought. The process worked as designed — a cheap, defensible "no," with $0 ever at risk.

About this project

A prediction-market research study by Ian Menachery. I tested whether a web-search LLM could beat Kalshi's prices net of fees, distrusted a promising but under-powered forecasting sample (n = 22), and built a statistically-powered 4,610-market behavioral study (event-clustered, net-of-overround) that carried the null — the favorite–longshot bias is real gross but fully consumed by the spread. I caught a look-ahead data leak that was faking a positive and killed a t = 2.6 result whose payoff skew invalidated the statistic, then deployed $0 capital — a complete, defensible "no edge" conclusion for under $7 in API spend. The value here is the judgment and the instrumentation, not an edge.
